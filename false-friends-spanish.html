Ponte

100 Italian False Friends for Spanish Speakers

If you already speak Spanish, most Italian vocabulary is a shortcut — but not all of it. These are the Italian words that look like Spanish and will make you confidently say the wrong thing. burro is not a donkey, salire is not to leave, and subire is not to go up. This is the full reference list, sorted by how badly each one will trip you up.

100 entries 22 high, 48 medium, 30 low
High danger Medium danger Low danger

High danger (22)

Confidently wrong — these will actively mislead you.

burro ≠ burro

burro means butter in Italian — but looks just like Spanish burro, which means donkey.

Ho bisogno del burro per fare la torta. I need the butter to make the cake.

💡 In Italy a burro goes on bread, not in a stable.

caldo ≠ caldo

caldo means hot (temperature) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish caldo, which means broth / soup stock.

Oggi fa molto caldo. It is very hot today.

💡 Italian caldo = heat; Spanish caldo = a bowl of soup.

largo ≠ largo

largo means wide, broad in Italian — but looks just like Spanish largo, which means long.

La strada è molto larga. The street is very wide.

💡 Italian largo = wide; Spanish largo = long. Reverse your mental map.

imbarazzato ≠ embarazada

imbarazzato means embarrassed in Italian — but looks just like Spanish embarazada, which means pregnant.

Era imbarazzato dopo aver sbagliato risposta. He was embarrassed after giving the wrong answer.

💡 Saying 'sono imbarazzato' means you're red-faced, not expecting a baby.

molestare ≠ molestar

molestare means to harass; to bother (in informal or mild contexts) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish molestar, which means to bother, to annoy.

Ha denunciato chi la molestava sul lavoro. She reported the person who was harassing her at work.

💡 Italian molestare overlaps with Spanish molestar at the mild end (to bother), but in formal or legal contexts it strongly implies harassment. When in doubt, say disturbare for mild annoyance.

borraccia ≠ borracho

borraccia means water bottle / canteen in Italian — but looks just like Spanish borracho, which means drunk.

Porta sempre la borraccia in borsa. She always carries a water bottle in her bag.

💡 Borraccia holds water; borracho has had too much of something else.

sensato ≠ sensible

sensato means sensible, reasonable in Italian — but looks just like Spanish sensible, which means sensitive.

È una decisione sensata. It is a sensible decision.

💡 Italian sensato = has good sense; Spanish sensible = feels things deeply. (Italian sensibile = Spanish sensible.)

firma ≠ firma

firma means signature in Italian — but looks just like Spanish firma, which means company / firm.

Metti la tua firma in fondo al documento. Put your signature at the bottom of the document.

💡 Italian firma = your name signed; Spanish firma = the company you work for.

argomento ≠ argumento

argomento means topic, subject in Italian — but looks just like Spanish argumento, which means argument (reasoning) / plot.

Qual è l'argomento della tua tesi? What is the topic of your thesis?

💡 Italian argomento = subject matter; for a heated debate, say discussione.

compromesso ≠ comprometido

compromesso means compromise in Italian — but looks just like Spanish comprometido, which means committed / engaged (to marry).

Abbiamo trovato un compromesso accettabile. We found an acceptable compromise.

💡 A compromesso is a deal struck; it doesn't mean you're engaged.

pretendere ≠ pretender

pretendere means to demand, to expect in Italian — but looks just like Spanish pretender, which means to try, to attempt; to court.

Pretende troppo dai suoi studenti. He demands too much from his students.

💡 Italian pretendere = high expectations or demands, not a polite attempt.

accidente ≠ accidente

accidente means darn! / damn! (exclamation); also: stroke (medical) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish accidente, which means accident.

Accidente! Ho dimenticato le chiavi. Darn! I forgot my keys.

💡 For a road accident in Italian say incidente, not accidente.

conveniente ≠ conveniente

conveniente means affordable, cheap (good value) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish conveniente, which means convenient, suitable.

Questo ristorante è molto conveniente. This restaurant is very affordable.

💡 Italian conveniente means a bargain; for 'convenient' say comodo.

educato ≠ educado

educato means polite, well-mannered in Italian — but looks just like Spanish educado, which means educated, well-raised.

È un ragazzo molto educato. He is a very polite boy.

💡 Italian educato = good manners only; for 'educated' (degree) say istruito.

eventuale ≠ eventual

eventuale means possible, potential (if it happens) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish eventual, which means eventual, final.

Considera eventuali problemi tecnici. Consider any possible technical problems.

💡 Italian eventuale = might happen; English 'eventual' = will happen eventually.

tenere ≠ tener

tenere means to hold, to keep in Italian — but looks just like Spanish tener, which means to have.

Tiene il bambino per mano. He holds the child by the hand.

💡 Italian tenere = to hold or keep; 'to have' is avere. 'Tieni!' means 'hold this!' or 'catch!' — not 'you have.'

magazzino ≠ magazine

magazzino means warehouse, storage room in Italian — but looks just like Spanish magazine, which means magazine (publication) — via English.

I prodotti sono nel magazzino. The products are in the warehouse.

💡 Italian magazzino = storage space; for a glossy magazine say rivista.

pavimento ≠ pavimento

pavimento means floor (indoor) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish pavimento, which means pavement, road surface.

Il pavimento della cucina è di marmo. The kitchen floor is marble.

💡 Italian pavimento = what you walk on inside; for road surface say asfalto.

morbido ≠ mórbido

morbido means soft, smooth in Italian — but looks just like Spanish mórbido, which means morbid (unhealthy fixation) — Spanish mórbido is rare.

Questo divano è molto morbido. This sofa is very soft.

💡 Italian morbido is cozy softness, nothing morbid about it.

brillo ≠ brillo

brillo means tipsy, slightly drunk in Italian — but looks just like Spanish brillo, which means shine, brilliance.

Dopo due bicchieri era già un po' brillo. After two glasses he was already a little tipsy.

💡 Italian brillo = a pleasant buzz; Spanish brillo = how your shoes look after polishing.

annoiato ≠ annoy

annoiato means bored in Italian — but looks just like Spanish annoy, which means to annoy (English root shared).

I bambini erano annoiati durante la lezione. The children were bored during the lesson.

💡 Italian annoiato = bored, not angry; if something bothers you, say mi dà fastidio.

folla ≠ folla

folla means crowd, throng in Italian — but looks just like Spanish folla, which means vulgar: third-person singular of follar.

C'era una folla enorme in piazza. There was an enormous crowd in the square.

💡 Italian folla is a perfectly ordinary word for a crowd. In Spanish, folla is a vulgar verb form — a jarring collision for Spanish speakers reading Italian news or event coverage.

Medium danger (48)

Easy to guess wrong in the moment.

preservativo ≠ preservativo

preservativo means condom (never used for food preservatives) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish preservativo, which means condom (in Spain, same as Italian); food preservative (in some Latin American varieties).

Il preservativo è un metodo contraccettivo. The condom is a contraceptive method.

💡 Italian preservativo = condom only — never a food additive (use conservante for that). Spanish speakers from Spain already know this meaning; in parts of Latin America where 'preservativo' means food preservative, the Italian usage may surprise.

sopportare ≠ soportar

sopportare means to tolerate, to put up with in Italian — but looks just like Spanish soportar, which means to bear, to support (structurally or emotionally).

Non sopporto il rumore del traffico. I can't stand the noise of the traffic.

💡 Meanings largely overlap — both express bearing something. Italian sopportare skews toward enduring something unpleasant; Spanish soportar covers structural support too. Close enough to read safely, but not interchangeable in production.

bravo ≠ brave

bravo means good, skilled, well done in Italian — but looks just like Spanish brave, which means brave, courageous (English); bravo ES = fierce/brave.

È una brava studentessa. She is a good student.

💡 Bravo in Italian praises skill, not courage — for brave say coraggioso.

roba ≠ ropa

roba means stuff, things, belongings in Italian — but looks just like Spanish ropa, which means clothing, clothes.

Hai lasciato tutta la tua roba in macchina. You left all your stuff in the car.

💡 Italian roba = general stuff; for clothes say vestiti or abiti.

salire ≠ salir

salire means to go up, to get on (a vehicle) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish salir, which means to leave, to go out.

Dobbiamo salire sull'autobus. We need to get on the bus.

💡 Italian salire = to climb or board; to leave in Italian is uscire.

camera ≠ cámara

camera means bedroom; (also) room in Italian — but looks just like Spanish cámara, which means camera; chamber.

La mia camera è al secondo piano. My bedroom is on the second floor.

💡 Camera in Italian is where you sleep, not what you photograph with.

cantina ≠ cantina

cantina means wine cellar; basement in Italian — but looks just like Spanish cantina, which means canteen, bar, roadside tavern.

Conserva il vino nella cantina. He stores wine in the cellar.

💡 Italian cantina is underground storage, not a drinks counter.

confrontare ≠ confrontar

confrontare means to compare in Italian — but looks just like Spanish confrontar, which means to confront.

Confronta i prezzi prima di comprare. Compare prices before buying.

💡 Italian confrontare = side-by-side comparison; to confront someone say affrontare.

palazzo ≠ palacio

palazzo means large apartment building; stately building in Italian — but looks just like Spanish palacio, which means palace (royal residence).

Abito in un palazzo del centro storico. I live in a building in the historic center.

💡 Italian palazzo often means your ordinary apartment block, not a royal palace.

prendere ≠ prender

prendere means to take, to get, to have (food/drink) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish prender, which means to arrest; to catch fire; to turn on.

Prendo un caffè, grazie. I'll have a coffee, thank you.

💡 Italian prendere is everyday 'take/get'; arrest in Italian is arrestare.

ufficio ≠ oficio

ufficio means office in Italian — but looks just like Spanish oficio, which means trade, craft, profession.

Lavoro in ufficio dalle nove alle cinque. I work in the office from nine to five.

💡 Italian ufficio = the room with desks; trade or craft is mestiere.

deludere ≠ delude

deludere means to disappoint in Italian — but looks just like Spanish delude, which means to trick, to deceive (English).

Non voglio deluderti. I don't want to disappoint you.

💡 Italian deludere = falling short of hopes; to deceive is ingannare.

successo ≠ suceso

successo means success; also past participle of succedere (it happened) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish suceso, which means event, occurrence.

Lo spettacolo è stato un grande successo. The show was a great success.

💡 Italian successo can mean triumph OR just 'it occurred'; context is key.

novella ≠ novela

novella means short story in Italian — but looks just like Spanish novela, which means novel (full-length fiction).

Ho letto una novella di Pirandello. I read a short story by Pirandello.

💡 Italian novella = short; for a full novel say romanzo.

gentile ≠ gentil

gentile means kind, nice in Italian — but looks just like Spanish gentil, which means graceful, elegant, courteous.

Sei molto gentile ad aiutarmi. You are very kind to help me.

💡 Italian gentile = warmly kind; it doesn't imply elegance of movement.

stanza ≠ estancia

stanza means room (in a house) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish estancia, which means stay; large ranch estate.

L'appartamento ha tre stanze. The apartment has three rooms.

💡 Italian stanza = any room; for a cattle ranch you need a different vocabulary.

tavola ≠ tabla

tavola means dining table; board (plank) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish tabla, which means board, plank; table (in data).

La cena è in tavola. Dinner is on the table.

💡 Italian tavola is where you eat; a data table is tabella.

recitare ≠ recitar

recitare means to act (theater/film); to recite in Italian — but looks just like Spanish recitar, which means to recite (poetry, text).

Ha recitato il ruolo del protagonista. He played the role of the protagonist.

💡 Italian recitare covers acting on stage, not just reciting lines from memory.

avanzare ≠ avanzar

avanzare means to advance; to have left over in Italian — but looks just like Spanish avanzar, which means to advance, to move forward.

È avanzato del pane dal pranzo. There was bread left over from lunch.

💡 Italian avanzare has the extra meaning of 'being left over' that Spanish avanzar lacks.

fissare ≠ fijar

fissare means to stare at; to fix; to book (an appointment) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish fijar, which means to fix, to set, to fasten.

Posso fissare un appuntamento per domani? Can I book an appointment for tomorrow?

💡 Italian fissare adds 'to book/schedule' and 'to stare' — broader than Spanish fijar.

giornale ≠ journal

giornale means newspaper in Italian — but looks just like Spanish journal, which means journal, diary (English).

Ho letto la notizia sul giornale. I read the news in the newspaper.

💡 Italian giornale = daily paper; a personal diary is diario.

sopra ≠ sobre

sopra means above, on top of, over in Italian — but looks just like Spanish sobre, which means envelope; about/on (preposition).

Il libro è sopra il tavolo. The book is on top of the table.

💡 Italian sopra = above; an envelope is busta and 'about' is di or riguardo a.

grosso ≠ grueso

grosso means large, big, fat in Italian — but looks just like Spanish grueso, which means thick, heavy.

È un grosso problema. It is a big problem.

💡 Italian grosso = big in general; Spanish grueso emphasizes thickness or girth.

locale ≠ local

locale means venue, bar, nightclub; local (adjective) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish local, which means local (adjective); premises.

Ci vediamo al locale stasera. Let's meet at the venue tonight.

💡 Italian locale as a noun usually means a bar or club, not just any premises.

manifesto ≠ manifiesto

manifesto means poster, billboard; (also) political manifesto in Italian — but looks just like Spanish manifiesto, which means manifest (document); manifesto.

C'è un manifesto del concerto sul muro. There is a concert poster on the wall.

💡 Italian manifesto most often means a street poster, not a political document.

parente ≠ parent

parente means relative, family member in Italian — but looks just like Spanish parent, which means mother or father (English).

Ho molti parenti in Sicilia. I have many relatives in Sicily.

💡 Italian parente = any relative; for mother/father say genitore/genitori.

famiglia ≠ familiar

famiglia means family in Italian — but looks just like Spanish familiar, which means family member (ES); familiar, well-known (EN).

La mia famiglia è numerosa. My family is large.

💡 Italian famiglia sounds like English 'familiar' but means the whole family unit. Spanish speakers: your familia = Italian famiglia — safe cognate. The trap is English.

annoiante ≠ annoying

annoiante means boring, tedious in Italian — but looks just like Spanish annoying, which means irritating (English).

Il film era piuttosto annoiante. The film was rather boring.

💡 Italian annoiante = makes you yawn, not makes you angry.

liceo ≠ liceo

liceo means high school (upper secondary, ages 14–19) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish liceo, which means lycée / high school (varies by country); also cultural club.

Ho frequentato il liceo classico. I attended a classical high school.

💡 Italian liceo is a specific academic track (not university). In some Spanish-speaking countries liceo is a cultural club, not a school — context matters.

corso ≠ curso

corso means main street; course (of study or meal) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish curso, which means course (of study), year (school year).

Il corso è affollato di negozi. The main street is full of shops.

💡 Italian corso doubles as the high street name in many Italian towns — a meaning Spanish curso never has.

sacco ≠ saco

sacco means bag, sack; (informal) a lot of in Italian — but looks just like Spanish saco, which means jacket, coat (in several Latin American varieties).

Ho un sacco di cose da fare. I have a lot of things to do.

💡 Italian sacco = bag or 'tons of'; it won't keep you warm in winter.

busta ≠ busto

busta means envelope; bag; pay packet (busta paga) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish busto, which means bust (sculpture of head and shoulders); upper body.

Metti la lettera nella busta. Put the letter in the envelope.

💡 Italian busta = what you seal and mail; Spanish busto = the marble torso in a museum.

primo ≠ primo

primo means first; first course of a meal (pasta/risotto) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish primo, which means cousin.

Come primo prendo le tagliatelle. For my first course I'll have tagliatelle.

💡 Italian primo = ordinal first or your pasta course; cousin is cugino.

patente ≠ patente

patente means driver's license in Italian — but looks just like Spanish patente, which means obvious, evident (adjective); also license plate (LATAM).

Ho appena preso la patente. I just got my driver's license.

💡 Italian patente = the card that lets you drive; obvious in Italian is ovvio.

rumore ≠ rumor

rumore means noise, sound in Italian — but looks just like Spanish rumor, which means rumor, gossip (English/Spanish).

Che rumore! Non riesco a dormire. What a noise! I can't sleep.

💡 Italian rumore = any sound; for gossip/rumor say voce or diceria.

tifoso ≠ tifoso / tifo

tifoso means fan, supporter (of a sports team) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish tifoso / tifo, which means typhoid (medical).

È un tifoso della Juventus. He is a Juventus fan.

💡 Italian tifoso comes from the passion that burns like a fever, but it just means a fan.

senso ≠ seno

senso means direction, sense, meaning in Italian — but looks just like Spanish seno, which means breast, bosom; bay (geography).

Questa strada è a senso unico. This street is one-way.

💡 Italian senso = direction or meaning ('senso unico' = one-way, 'non ha senso' = it doesn't make sense). Spanish seno is anatomical or geographic — one letter apart, worlds apart.

finire ≠ finar

finire means to finish; to end up (somewhere) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish finar, which means to die (archaic/poetic Spanish).

Dove sei finito? Where did you end up?

💡 Italian finire is everyday 'to finish'; the 'end up somewhere' usage (dove sei finito?) has no clean Spanish equivalent.

stanco ≠ estanco

stanco means tired in Italian — but looks just like Spanish estanco, which means tobacco shop / state monopoly store.

Sono molto stanco dopo la maratona. I am very tired after the marathon.

💡 Italian stanco = fatigue; Spanish estanco is where you buy cigarettes.

dimenticare ≠ desmentir

dimenticare means to forget in Italian — but looks just like Spanish desmentir, which means to deny, to refute.

Ho dimenticato il portafoglio a casa. I forgot my wallet at home.

💡 Italian dimenticare = to forget; the visual resemblance to Spanish 'desmentir' (to deny) is partial but enough to cause a double-take.

andare ≠ andar

andare means to go in Italian — but looks just like Spanish andar, which means to walk; to function/work (of machines); to be going on.

Vado a scuola ogni mattina. I go to school every morning.

💡 Italian andare = to go (general movement); walking is camminare. Spanish andar covers both walking and how a machine works — andare does neither.

fermo ≠ firme

fermo means still, stationary, stopped in Italian — but looks just like Spanish firme, which means firm, solid, steady; 'at attention!' (military).

Stai fermo un momento! Stay still for a moment!

💡 Italian fermo = not moving; Spanish firme = strong and steady. 'Stai fermo!' = don't move; '¡firme!' = stand at attention.

notizia ≠ noticia

notizia means piece of news; (plural) news in Italian — but looks just like Spanish noticia, which means piece of news.

Hai sentito la notizia? Did you hear the news?

💡 One notizia = one piece of news; for 'the news' (broadcast) say telegiornale.

coinvolto ≠ convulso

coinvolto means involved in Italian — but looks just like Spanish convulso, which means convulsive, turbulent.

È coinvolto nel progetto da mesi. He has been involved in the project for months.

💡 Coinvolto shares letters with 'convulsed/convolved' but simply means involved.

fermare ≠ firmar

fermare means to stop, to halt in Italian — but looks just like Spanish firmar, which means to sign (a document).

Fermati qui, per favore. Stop here, please.

💡 Italian fermare = press the brakes; to sign is firmare (different word, same letters rearranged).

contare ≠ contar

contare means to count (numbers); to matter; to rely on in Italian — but looks just like Spanish contar, which means to count (numbers); to tell, to narrate.

Contiamo su di te. We are counting on you.

💡 Italian contare = to count AND to matter or rely on ('conta su di me!' = count on me). Spanish contar also = to tell a story — Italian uses raccontare for narrating. 'Raccontami' = tell me; 'contami' sounds odd.

pure ≠ puro

pure means also, even, moreover; go ahead, feel free in Italian — but looks just like Spanish puro, which means pure, clean; cigar.

Vieni pure a cena con noi! Feel free to come to dinner with us!

💡 Italian pure (adverb) = also, even, or 'by all means/feel free' — 'vieni pure' = go ahead and come. Spanish puro (adjective) = pure or a cigar. Italian puro as an adjective also means pure, but the adverb usage is the trap.

rendere ≠ rendir

rendere means to give back, to return; to make, to render in Italian — but looks just like Spanish rendir, which means to yield, to surrender; to exhaust; to perform (rendir un examen = to sit an exam).

Questo colore ti rende bene. This color suits you / looks good on you.

💡 Italian rendere = to give back OR to make something ('rendere felice' = to make happy). Spanish rendir = to yield, perform, or surrender. Returning a book in Italian = 'rendere il libro'; in Spanish you'd say 'devolver'.

Low danger (30)

Minor mix-ups, rarely a real misunderstanding.

libreria ≠ librería

libreria means bookshop; bookcase in Italian — but looks just like Spanish librería, which means bookshop (same as Italian in most regions).

Ho comprato il romanzo in libreria. I bought the novel at the bookshop.

💡 For Spanish speakers, Italian libreria and Spanish librería share the same meaning (bookshop). The false friend is with English 'library' — the Italian word for that is biblioteca.

attuale ≠ actual

attuale means current, present-day in Italian — but looks just like Spanish actual, which means current, present (same as Italian).

La situazione attuale è complessa. The current situation is complex.

💡 For Spanish speakers, attuale/actual share the same meaning (current/present) — a safe cognate. The only trap is English 'actual' (= real/genuine), which in Italian is reale or vero.

tardi ≠ tarde

tardi means late (time of day or lateness) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish tarde, which means afternoon; late.

Sono arrivato tardi alla riunione. I arrived late to the meeting.

💡 Italian tardi and Spanish tarde both mean 'late' — a shared meaning. The difference is that Spanish tarde also means 'afternoon', which in Italian is pomeriggio.

spettacolo ≠ espectáculo

spettacolo means show, performance, spectacle in Italian — but looks just like Spanish espectáculo, which means spectacle, show.

Lo spettacolo inizia alle otto. The show starts at eight.

💡 Nearly identical to Spanish espectáculo — same root, same meaning. A safe cognate. Italians also use it informally for anything impressive ('che spettacolo!' = wow!).

sensibile ≠ sensible

sensibile means sensitive in Italian — but looks just like Spanish sensible, which means sensitive (same as Italian).

È una persona molto sensibile. She is a very sensitive person.

💡 Italian sensibile and Spanish sensible both mean 'sensitive' — a safe cognate. The only trap is English 'sensible' (= reasonable/practical), which in Italian is sensato or ragionevole.

divertire ≠ divertir

divertire means to amuse, to entertain in Italian — but looks just like Spanish divertir, which means to amuse, to entertain.

Il clown divertiva i bambini. The clown was entertaining the children.

💡 Very close to Spanish divertir — a safe cognate. The reflexive divertirsi = divertirse = to enjoy oneself/have fun. No real trap for Spanish speakers.

orario ≠ horario

orario means timetable, schedule, opening hours in Italian — but looks just like Spanish horario, which means timetable, schedule.

Qual è l'orario del treno? What is the train timetable?

💡 Essentially identical to Spanish horario — a safe cognate. Italians also use orario for store opening hours, which aligns with how Spanish horario works.

simpatico ≠ simpático

simpatico means likable, friendly, nice (personality) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish simpático, which means nice, likable (same as Italian).

Il professore è molto simpatico. The professor is very likable.

💡 Italian simpatico and Spanish simpático share the same meaning — a safe cognate. The only danger is English 'sympathetic' (= compassionate), which is very different from both.

assistere ≠ asistir

assistere means to attend (an event); to assist in Italian — but looks just like Spanish asistir, which means to attend; to assist.

Ho assistito alla conferenza. I attended the conference.

💡 Meanings closely align with Spanish asistir — both mean to attend and to assist. A mostly safe cognate; the subtle difference is that Italian 'assistere a' emphasizes passive presence rather than active participation.

saluto ≠ saludo

saluto means greeting; farewell in Italian — but looks just like Spanish saludo, which means greeting.

Ti mando tanti saluti. I send you many greetings.

💡 Almost identical — Italians also use saluto for a farewell, which saludo rarely covers.

fresco ≠ fresco

fresco means cool (temperature); fresh in Italian — but looks just like Spanish fresco, which means cool; fresh; brazen (figurative).

Fa fresco stasera. It is cool this evening.

💡 Nearly the same, but Spanish fresco can mean cheeky/forward — Italian uses sfacciato for that.

ricco ≠ rico

ricco means rich, wealthy; rich (food/flavor) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish rico, which means rich; delicious.

Questo tiramisù è molto ricco. This tiramisù is very rich.

💡 Italian ricco = wealthy or flavorful, just like Spanish rico — minor usage overlap. Spanish rico also means delicious; Italian uses buonissimo or squisito for that.

lungo ≠ luengo

lungo means long (length); tall (person, informal) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish luengo, which means long (archaic Spanish).

È un viaggio lungo. It is a long journey.

💡 Italian lungo = long; remember largo = wide — the two are often confused by Spanish speakers.

vecchio ≠ viejo

vecchio means old (age or objects) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish viejo, which means old; (informal) mate/buddy.

Questa casa è molto vecchia. This house is very old.

💡 Italian vecchio = old; you won't use it to call a friend 'buddy' — say amico.

presto ≠ presto

presto means soon; early; quickly in Italian — but looks just like Spanish presto, which means quickly (music term borrowed from Italian).

Ci vediamo presto! See you soon!

💡 Italian presto = soon or early in time; it's not about speed the way English 'presto' implies.

macchina ≠ máquina

macchina means machine; car (in everyday speech) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish máquina, which means machine, engine (not usually a car).

Ho preso la macchina per andare al lavoro. I took the car to go to work.

💡 Italian macchina doubles as the everyday word for car — 'prendo la macchina' = I'll take the car. Spanish uses coche, carro, or auto instead.

subito ≠ súbito

subito means immediately, right away in Italian — but looks just like Spanish súbito, which means sudden, abrupt (adjective).

Arrivo subito! I'll be right there!

💡 Italian subito = do it now; Spanish súbito is an adjective meaning sudden.

antico ≠ antiguo

antico means ancient, old (of historical things) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish antiguo, which means ancient, old, former.

Roma è piena di monumenti antichi. Rome is full of ancient monuments.

💡 Very close — Italian antico rarely means 'former' the way Spanish antiguo can (my former boss = mi antiguo jefe; Italian would say ex-capo).

sentire ≠ sentir

sentire means to hear; to feel; to smell in Italian — but looks just like Spanish sentir, which means to feel; to regret (lo siento = I'm sorry).

Senti che profumo! Can you smell that fragrance!

💡 Italian sentire covers hearing, feeling, AND smelling — broader than Spanish sentir. 'Ti sento' on the phone = 'I can hear you'; 'sento freddo' = 'I feel cold'; 'sento un odore' = 'I smell something.'

capire ≠ caber

capire means to understand in Italian — but looks just like Spanish caber, which means to fit (inside something).

Non capisco cosa vuoi dire. I don't understand what you mean.

💡 Italian capire = comprehension; Spanish caber = does it fit in the box.

piacere ≠ placer

piacere means to please; to like (mi piace = I like it); pleasure; nice to meet you in Italian — but looks just like Spanish placer, which means pleasure (noun); to please (archaic/formal verb).

Piacere di conoscerti. Nice to meet you.

💡 Italian piacere is the everyday verb for liking ('mi piace' = I like it) and the standard greeting 'piacere!' = nice to meet you. Spanish uses gustar as the equivalent everyday verb; placer is mostly a formal noun.

pasto ≠ pasto

pasto means meal in Italian — but looks just like Spanish pasto, which means grass, pasture.

Facciamo tre pasti al giorno. We have three meals a day.

💡 Italian pasto = what you eat; Spanish pasto = what cows eat.

posta ≠ posta

posta means mail, post office in Italian — but looks just like Spanish posta, which means relay station (archaic); on purpose (a posta = IT).

Devo andare alla posta. I need to go to the post office.

💡 Italian posta = letters and postal service; also used in 'l'ho fatto apposta' (I did it on purpose).

girare ≠ girar

girare means to turn; to wander; to shoot (a film) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish girar, which means to turn, to rotate.

Hanno girato il film a Venezia. They filmed the movie in Venice.

💡 Italian girare adds 'to shoot a film' and 'to roam around' beyond Spanish girar.

guardare ≠ guardar

guardare means to look at, to watch in Italian — but looks just like Spanish guardar, which means to keep, to save, to store.

Guarda che bel tramonto! Look at what a beautiful sunset!

💡 Italian guardare = direct your gaze; Spanish guardar = put it away safely.

gomma ≠ goma

gomma means rubber; eraser; chewing gum; car tire in Italian — but looks just like Spanish goma, which means rubber; eraser; glue/adhesive (LATAM); elastic band.

Ho bucato una gomma sull'autostrada. I got a flat tire on the highway.

💡 Italian gomma extends to car tires ('ho bucato la gomma' = flat tire) — a meaning Spanish goma doesn't usually have. The rubber and eraser meanings are shared.

porto ≠ puerto

porto means port, harbor; I carry in Italian — but looks just like Spanish puerto, which means port, harbor.

La nave è entrata nel porto. The ship entered the harbor.

💡 Same harbor meaning — but Italian porto also means 'I carry', a handy double use.

vicino ≠ vecino

vicino means near, close; neighbor in Italian — but looks just like Spanish vecino, which means neighbor, resident.

La farmacia è vicino alla stazione. The pharmacy is near the station.

💡 Italian vicino does the work of both Spanish 'cercano' (nearby) and 'vecino' (neighbor).

partire ≠ partir

partire means to depart, to leave in Italian — but looks just like Spanish partir, which means to leave; to split/divide.

Il treno parte alle dieci. The train leaves at ten.

💡 Italian partire = only to depart; to split something say dividere or tagliare.

scala ≠ escala

scala means staircase; ladder; scale (music/map) in Italian — but looks just like Spanish escala, which means scale; stopover (flight).

Sali le scale fino al terzo piano. Go up the stairs to the third floor.

💡 Italian scala most commonly means the staircase; Spanish escala is usually a transit stop.
